# Why I Started Writing Everything Down

For about three years after being discharged from the hospital, I continued to experience a significant decline in my ability to learn new things and recall important information whenever my fatigue became more severe in the afternoon or my vital signs, such as blood pressure, dropped due to fluctuations throughout the day.

At the time, I did not even recognize that these changes were happening. I spent many days blaming myself and wondering, "Why can't I remember something today that I learned just yesterday?"

That was when I began a habit of writing down the date and time at the beginning of every note, recording things I noticed and appointments I needed to remember by hand, and reviewing my notes several times a day.

Much of what I reviewed gradually became part of my memory. However, when I opened my notebook later, I often came across notes that I had completely forgotten writing. I would find myself thinking, "Did I really write this?"

Each time this happened, I sometimes felt anxious and even desperate, wondering what would happen if I never recovered.

During that period, I began thinking about ways to address my difficulties from two different perspectives.

The first was whether there might be a medical treatment that could improve my condition.

While researching various possibilities, I learned about transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS). However, since I am currently experiencing improvements through practical adjustments in my daily life, I regard it as an option to consider if I ever need it in the future.

The second approach was to create systems that could help compensate for my memory difficulties.

While living in Vietnam, I tried various ways to avoid forgetting things, such as asking people to remind me of important details and sending emails to myself.

However, a story I once heard from an older person significantly changed the way I thought about memory.

For many years, this person had followed a consistent routine of keeping their belongings in the same places and putting them in the same pockets whenever they went out.

But the more strictly they followed this routine, the fewer opportunities they had to actively recall where things were. As a result, they felt that they had actually become more forgetful.

They gradually began changing where they placed their belongings. Each time, they would consciously ask themselves, "Where should I put this today?" and "Will I be able to remember where I left it later?"

They told me that, through this practice, they began to remember things more often.

After hearing this story, I began to think that perhaps I should focus not only on "not forgetting," but also on developing the habit of actively trying to remember.

Sometimes, I would deliberately put my wallet somewhere different from its usual place and enjoy trying to recall where I had left it, almost as if it were a game.

At first, I struggled even with the little "tricks" I had set up for myself. But as I repeated the exercise, I gradually became better at remembering, and small successes began to accumulate.

Looking back, I feel that my notes served not only as a tool to help me avoid forgetting things, but also as a support system that helped me strengthen my ability to recall information.

I still use notes today, but rather than relying on them to remember everything for me, I use them as a way to support and develop my own memory.

What I learned from this experience is that memory is not something that can only be lost. There may also be aspects of it that we can rebuild by finding better ways to use it in our everyday lives.
