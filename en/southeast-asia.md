# What I Learned After Letting Go of My Former Career and Finding a New Way to Work

*Finding a New Path After Surgery*

After undergoing adrenalectomy, I continued rehabilitation with the goal of returning to my previous job.

However, medical care at the time was not as advanced as it is today, particularly in the management of blood pressure during surgery and the care provided around the time of an operation. In my case, I was left with a range of persistent symptoms that affected my daily life.

I experienced severe dizziness whenever I turned my head or neck, and sometimes one of my legs would suddenly feel as though it had lost its strength while I was walking. I struggled with simple mental arithmetic and could no longer remember short sequences of numbers. When I tried to write, I sometimes found myself unable to recall familiar kanji characters or even words.

Things I had always taken for granted had suddenly become difficult.

At a neurosurgery department, I was told that the brain may be able to form new neural pathways to compensate for damaged areas, and that continued rehabilitation could lead to improvement.

I consulted doctors at several hospitals and continued searching for ways to regain as much of my former life as possible.

After about a year, however, I came to the conclusion that returning to my previous way of working would be difficult.

The main reason was the extreme fatigue I experienced, particularly in the afternoon. I found it difficult to maintain a regular nine-to-five work schedule consistently.

---

Around that time, an acquaintance approached me with an opportunity to take over a business in Southeast Asia.

Accepting the opportunity, however, would require me to learn the local language and become familiar with the healthcare system there.

I therefore decided to begin by working as a medical interpreter at a hospital serving foreign patients, initially for a planned period of one year.

Looking back, I now feel that this was less a period of employment than a training period in which I learned how to manage my own health.

---

While living and working there, I encountered a different approach to healthcare from what I had known in Japan.

Alongside conventional Western medical examinations and tests, there was a natural emphasis on observing changes in a person's health and lifestyle over time and making adjustments accordingly.

In addition to regular medical consultations, I also had opportunities to receive traditional herbal medicine-based care and dietary guidance tailored to changes in my condition.

The Australian doctor who treated me was particularly interested in my overall lifestyle, not just my test results.

He would ask questions such as:

- What kind of work are you doing?
- Are you getting enough sleep?
- At what time of day does your fatigue tend to appear?
- How is your condition affecting your everyday life?

Today, the term QOL, or Quality of Life, is widely recognized. But at the time, this was my first experience of healthcare that looked beyond medical test results to consider how I was actually living.

---

Of course, it would not be possible to reproduce the healthcare I experienced in Southeast Asia exactly as it was in Japan.

The medical systems and cultures are different.

Nevertheless, I came to feel that many of the principles I learned through that experience could also be applied within Japan's healthcare system.

For example:

- Keeping a record of the times of day when fatigue occurs
- Observing how food and changes in health are related
- Exploring ways of working that do not place excessive demands on the body
- Looking at a person's overall life, rather than focusing only on individual symptoms

These are small, practical steps that can help us better understand our own health and find ways to live more comfortably.

---

This QOL section is not intended to explain diseases themselves. Instead, it draws on my personal experience to explore a different question:

How can we build a life that works for us while living with an illness?

More than 20 years have passed since my surgery, yet new challenges continue to arise.

Some of these changes are also related to aging.

Even so, I have come to believe that by making adjustments to suit our circumstances at each stage of life, it is possible to maintain our quality of life and continue living in a way that feels meaningful to us.
