# When a Tumor Develops in the Only Remaining Adrenal Gland After Contralateral Adrenalectomy

Tumor development in the remaining adrenal gland following contralateral adrenalectomy.

The challenge in this situation is how to balance life expectancy and quality of life (QOL) when a tumor develops in the only remaining adrenal gland after the opposite adrenal gland has already been removed.

I believe that treatment options should not be limited to surgery from the outset. Instead, the situation should be evaluated comprehensively from the following five perspectives:

Accurately assess whether the tumor is malignant and whether metastasis is present.

Preserve as much of the remaining adrenal function as possible.

Consider local treatment options other than surgery.

Explore systemic treatments if metastasis or disease progression is confirmed.

Minimize the impact of treatment on adrenal function and everyday life.

One particularly important point is that the possibility of malignancy does not automatically mean that the entire remaining adrenal gland must be removed immediately.

Furthermore, pheochromocytoma cannot always be reliably classified as benign or malignant based solely on its histological appearance. The presence or absence of metastasis is therefore an important factor in determining its malignant potential.

1. What Needs to Be Established First: Is It Really a Pheochromocytoma?

The first step is to determine the exact nature of the tumor identified in the remaining adrenal gland.

Adrenal tumors include benign adenomas containing fat, pheochromocytomas that produce catecholamines, and tumors that invade surrounding tissues or spread to distant organs. Since the appropriate treatment differs depending on the type of tumor, careful evaluation using imaging studies and hormonal tests is essential.

In particular, it can be difficult to distinguish benign from malignant pheochromocytoma based solely on histological appearance. Evidence of metastasis to the liver, lungs, bones, or lymph nodes is therefore an important consideration.

There is also no guarantee that the current tumor has the same characteristics as the pheochromocytoma removed in the past.

For this reason, it is important to establish the exact nature of the current tumor rather than assuming that it represents malignant transformation. The possibility of an entirely different type of benign adrenal tumor should also be considered.

2. Treatment Options When Malignancy Is Suspected

There are six broad treatment approaches to consider.

① Active Surveillance and Careful Monitoring

Prioritizing the preservation of adrenal function

If the tumor is small, shows little change on imaging, and is not associated with hormonal abnormalities or symptoms, careful monitoring without immediate surgery may be an option.

The main examinations include:

MRI and CT scans to assess tumor size and characteristics.

Plasma or urinary fractionated metanephrines.

Monitoring of blood pressure and heart rate.

Functional imaging studies when necessary.

Evaluation of adrenal function, including cortisol and ACTH levels.

Treatment decisions should take into account not only tumor size, but also its growth rate, hormone production, and imaging characteristics.

The fact that follow-up examinations are currently scheduled every six months does not necessarily mean that the tumor has become malignant.

② Partial Adrenalectomy

An especially important treatment option in this situation

This is one of the options I would particularly like my treating physician to consider.

In a conventional adrenalectomy, the entire adrenal gland is removed. In a partial adrenalectomy, only the tumor is removed while preserving as much healthy adrenal cortex as possible.

Potential benefits include:

Maintaining the ability to produce adrenal hormones naturally.

Potentially avoiding lifelong dependence on hormone replacement therapy.

Reducing the risk of adrenal insufficiency and adrenal crisis.

Preserving greater freedom and flexibility in everyday life.

However, there are also important limitations and risks:

Complete removal of the tumor may not always be possible.

The tumor may recur in the remaining adrenal tissue.

There is no guarantee that the preserved tissue will produce sufficient hormones.

When malignancy is strongly suspected, complete tumor removal may need to take priority.

The Japanese Society of Endocrinology's revised 2025 guidelines also discuss consideration of partial adrenalectomy in patients with pheochromocytoma who are at risk of bilateral disease or who have already undergone removal of the opposite adrenal gland. However, the evidence supporting this recommendation remains limited.

In other words, when one adrenal gland has already been removed, surgical decisions must take into account circumstances that differ significantly from those in an ordinary case of pheochromocytoma.

③ Local Tumor Ablation and Other Local Treatments

If removing the entire adrenal gland through surgery is something we want to avoid, local treatment may also be worth considering, depending on the tumor's location and size.

Representative approaches include:

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA), which destroys tumor tissue using heat.

Cryoablation, which destroys tumor tissue by freezing it.

Radiation therapy, which uses radiation to control tumor growth.

These treatments are not suitable for every adrenal tumor.

Pheochromocytomas, in particular, may release substances that cause sudden increases in blood pressure. Therefore, procedures such as tumor ablation require careful preparation and specialist management.

Although local treatments may offer the possibility of preserving adrenal function, they are not established as standard curative treatments for pheochromocytoma in all situations.

④ Total Adrenalectomy

Complete removal of the remaining adrenal gland

This is an option when complete tumor removal must take priority.

However, it creates a particularly significant issue in this situation.

If the only remaining adrenal gland is completely removed, the body will generally lose its ability to produce sufficient adrenal cortical hormones on its own.

As a result, lifelong management will be necessary, including:

Glucocorticoid replacement, such as hydrocortisone.

Mineralocorticoid replacement, such as fludrocortisone, when necessary.

Increased steroid doses during fever, infections, surgery, and other periods of physical stress.

Preparation for emergency self-injection when required.

Knowledge and ongoing management to prevent adrenal crisis.

Adrenal insufficiency is not simply a matter of taking medication every day.

If medication cannot be absorbed because of vomiting or diarrhea, or if the body's demand for hormones increases due to infection or injury, an adrenal crisis may occur. This can involve a sudden drop in blood pressure and impaired consciousness, potentially becoming life-threatening.

On the other hand, with appropriate hormone replacement and adequate preparation for emergencies, it is possible to maintain daily life even after both adrenal glands have been removed.

Therefore, total adrenalectomy is not a treatment that must be avoided under all circumstances. However, in this particular situation, its long-term consequences must be fully understood before making a decision.

Three Particularly Important Issues in This Situation

The following points take into account the medical history and circumstances I have described.

Issue A: How Much of the Remaining Adrenal Gland Can Be Preserved?

Since one adrenal gland was removed more than 20 years ago, the remaining gland is not simply the other half of a pair of organs.

It is the only adrenal gland left to produce the hormones essential for maintaining life.

Therefore, when considering treatment options, the following priorities may be worth discussing:

Establish the nature of the tumor and assess the risk of progression.

Determine whether complete tumor removal is necessary.

Ask a specialist to evaluate whether partial adrenalectomy is technically feasible.

If partial adrenalectomy is difficult, explore alternatives such as local treatment.

If total adrenalectomy becomes necessary, make the decision with a clear understanding of the long-term management of adrenal insufficiency.

In particular, the feasibility of partial adrenalectomy depends on several factors, including the tumor's location, its relationship to surrounding blood vessels, the amount of healthy adrenal cortex that can be preserved, and the degree of suspicion for malignancy.

Issue B: Distinguishing Tumor Malignancy from Its Growth Rate

Even if a tumor is suspected of being malignant, not all tumors progress at the same rate.

For example, the following findings have different clinical implications:

The tumor is gradually increasing in size.

Hormone production is increasing.

Invasion into surrounding tissues is suspected.

Metastases are found in the lymph nodes, lungs, liver, or bones.

The mere presence of a tumor does not automatically mean that immediate total adrenalectomy is necessary.

On the other hand, if clear evidence of invasion or metastasis is found, complete tumor removal or systemic treatment may need to take priority.

Issue C: Accurately Assessing the Current Function of the Remaining Adrenal Gland

The test results I have previously discussed include metanephrines, noradrenaline, dopamine, cortisol, and ACTH.

These measurements provide important information when considering future treatment options.

In particular, two separate questions need to be evaluated:

Is the tumor producing excessive amounts of catecholamines?

Is the remaining adrenal gland producing sufficient hormones to maintain essential bodily functions?

These are two different medical issues and should not be confused with one another.

3. Questions to Discuss with My Treating Physician

At my next appointment, I would like to have the following questions prepared so that the available treatment options can be discussed more concretely.

Can the current fat-containing tumor be considered an adrenal adenoma, or does the possibility of pheochromocytoma remain?

Based on the tumor's current size, location, and imaging characteristics, would partial adrenalectomy be technically feasible?

If malignancy is suspected in the future, what criteria would determine whether partial or total adrenalectomy is appropriate?

Could adrenal-function-preserving local treatments, such as radiofrequency ablation or cryoablation, be considered?

If total adrenalectomy becomes necessary, how would adrenal insufficiency be managed after surgery?

Would genetic testing for pheochromocytoma provide useful information in this situation?

Balancing Tumor Control with Quality of Life

Stage 1: Establish an Accurate Understanding of the Current Condition

Determine the nature of the tumor, its growth rate, hormone production, and whether metastasis is present.

Stage 2: Explore Every Reasonable Opportunity to Preserve Adrenal Function

Consider approaches such as partial adrenalectomy and local treatments that may help maintain the function of the remaining adrenal gland.

Stage 3: Prioritize Tumor Control When Necessary

If the clinical situation requires it, consider treatments aimed at protecting long-term survival, including total adrenalectomy, radiopharmaceutical therapy, and chemotherapy.

Stage 4: Protect Quality of Life After Treatment

Provide long-term care that takes into account hormone management, everyday activities, physical functioning, and psychological well-being.

Ultimately, the goal should not be simply to remove the tumor, but to find the most appropriate balance between controlling the disease, preserving adrenal function, and maintaining a sustainable quality of life.
