# Work & Social Reintegration

One of the first challenges I faced after surgery was figuring out how I would work and support myself going forward.

At the time, I had serious concerns about my physical stamina and overall health, and I lacked the confidence to return to my previous job. My doctor had also explained that there was a significant possibility of a tumor developing in my remaining adrenal gland in the future. I was advised that avoiding severe stress was more important for prevention than simply limiting alcohol and tobacco.

As a result, I began to wonder whether I would be able to manage a conventional nine-to-five job.

Around that time, I found myself thinking about Little Saigon, a Vietnamese neighborhood in California that I had often visited while working there in my previous job. For some reason, I had always felt comfortable around people from southern Vietnam. I could relax, let my guard down, and simply be myself in their company. When I began thinking about what kind of environment might be less stressful for me after treatment, that memory was the first thing that came to mind.

Coincidentally, an opportunity to work in Vietnam came along at about the same time. Back then, university graduates in Vietnam earned starting salaries of around 30,000 yen a month. I thought, rather optimistically in retrospect, that I might be able to hire a Vietnamese person who spoke English and run a business without having to do everything myself.

More than anything, I think I wanted to put some distance between myself and the anxiety surrounding my illness and future. I wanted to leave Japan, where I had often felt expected to maintain a highly disciplined approach to life, and try living among people with a more relaxed and flexible outlook. With those thoughts in mind, I decided to move to southern Vietnam.

The circumstances that led me to relocate may not be particularly relevant to many readers. However, I believe that anyone who has experienced a serious illness and watched their career and future plans fall apart may understand the feeling of wanting to escape from the life they once knew.

Today, the environment surrounding people with cancer has changed considerably compared with 20 years ago. There is greater understanding of the challenges of working while undergoing treatment, and there are more options for how people can work.

I also experienced job hunting in middle age. During that time, I found that qualities often associated with Japanese workers, such as diligence, the ability to work cooperatively, and a strong sense of responsibility, were generally well regarded by employers overseas. I did not find myself at a disadvantage simply because I was applying to companies in Europe, the United States, or Southeast Asia.

Even if an illness makes it difficult to return to your previous way of working, that does not mean you have run out of options. Returning to your former job is not the only way forward. I believe that finding a way of working that suits your health, personal values, and circumstances can also be a meaningful form of social reintegration.
