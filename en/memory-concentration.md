# When I Couldn’t Understand What Was Happening

Before surgery, I rarely forgot appointments or plans I had made.

However, some time after surgery, I began to forget the dates and times of important meetings more frequently.

What surprised me even more was that I sometimes forgot not only the meeting details, but the fact that I had agreed to meet in the first place.

The day before a meeting, I would receive a message from the other person confirming our plans and find myself thinking:

“Did I really make that appointment?”

I would then hurriedly check my schedule, trying to figure out what had happened. This happened more than once.

At the time, I was struggling with questions I could not answer on my own.

- Was something wrong with my brain?
- Had I developed a mental health condition?
- Would I ever return to normal?

Looking back, however, I realize that this was not simply a matter of forgetting things.

When I made arrangements over the phone, I was already experiencing severe fatigue.

I was so exhausted that even thinking had become difficult. As a result, the details of the conversation had not properly registered in my mind.

In other words, I had not necessarily forgotten the information. I may never have been able to form a lasting memory of it in the first place.

---

## Why I Didn’t Recognize the Connection with Fatigue

At the time, I was experiencing a level of fatigue and exhaustion that I had never known before.

However, it was not the kind of fatigue that was easy to recognize, like the tiredness that comes with a cold or a lack of sleep.

My condition fluctuated considerably throughout the day. There were even periods when I could function relatively well, so I could not see the full picture of my fatigue, even in my own experience.

As a result, I did not realize that all of the following difficulties might be related to fatigue:

- Finding it difficult to maintain concentration while driving in the evening
- Feeling reluctant to make the journey home after going out
- Becoming unable to get up and leave a restaurant or other place after sitting there for a long time
- Finding it difficult to think things through

Looking back, I can see that my mistakes and poor decisions tended to occur in the evening.

At the time, however, I could not even recognize that common pattern.

---

## Gradually, Things Began to Make Sense

About one to two years after surgery, I began to wonder:

“Could fatigue be the reason I make more mistakes at night?”

From that point on, I started making a conscious effort to:

- Keep detailed notes
- Record appointments and plans as soon as they were made
- Keep track of my vital signs
- Observe changes in my physical condition

As I continued these practices, patterns of fatigue that had previously been difficult to recognize gradually became clearer.

Later, a brain MRI revealed findings that raised the possibility of changes associated with the rise in blood pressure during surgery.

It was only around that time that I began to understand that the many difficulties I had experienced after surgery might have been closely related to changes in my brain, rather than being a matter of laziness or simply needing to change my attitude.

---

## For Those Experiencing Similar Concerns

If you have noticed changes after surgery, such as:

- Forgetting appointments
- Having difficulty concentrating
- Finding it hard to organize your thoughts
- Suddenly feeling as though your brain stops working in the evening

These symptoms alone do not necessarily mean that you have dementia or a mental health condition.

I myself spent a long time feeling anxious without understanding what was causing my difficulties.

One perspective that may be helpful is to consider the possibility that fatigue could be significantly affecting how your brain functions, and to begin observing changes in your own condition.

Keeping a record of your health and making notes of your experiences can be a valuable way to better understand what is happening.

---

At the time, I was suffering because I could not understand why these things were happening to me.

Looking back now, I realize that what I really needed was not more willpower or greater effort, but a better understanding of my own condition.

I think the most difficult period was the time when I continued blaming myself without knowing what was causing my problems.

---

Back then, I could not even organize my thoughts well enough to explain what was happening to me. If AI had been as accessible as it is today, I might have turned to it as someone who would listen, even when I could not put my thoughts into words.

---

I never imagined that someone who would patiently listen to me, even when my thoughts were disorganized, might one day exist in a form other than another human being.

It is remarkable how much the world can change over a lifetime.

Those who are facing similar difficulties today may encounter technologies and forms of support twenty years from now that they cannot even imagine at present.

That possibility alone, I believe, is a good reason to hold on to a little hope for the future.
