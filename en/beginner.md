# My Pheochromocytoma Journey: From Diagnosis to Treatment

## It All Started with a Routine Checkup

About 20 years ago, when I was 36, a routine health check revealed that I had high blood pressure.

For a while, my condition was simply monitored. I think I took it somewhat lightly, assuming that a little extra care would be enough to keep things under control.

---

## Being Told I Might Have an Adrenal Tumor

A few years later, my blood pressure readings worsened as my physical condition changed, and I underwent more detailed examinations.

The results revealed a suspected tumor in my adrenal gland.

I was also told that it would not be possible to determine whether the tumor was benign or malignant until after surgery.

Based on the examination results, I was informed that there was a high likelihood of malignancy.

After surgery, the pathology results confirmed that the tumor was malignant. It was also around this time that the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma appears to have been confirmed.

---

## Choosing to Undergo Adrenalectomy

*A Proven but More Invasive Approach or a Minimally Invasive Alternative*

I was presented with two main surgical options: a well-established procedure that involved a greater physical burden, and laparoscopic surgery, which was still relatively new at the time.

As I learned about the success and survival rates and began researching the options myself, I went through several days of feeling deeply discouraged.

Then I noticed that what I was experiencing seemed remarkably similar to the stages people go through when coming to terms with death.

Recognizing this helped me put my feelings into perspective, at least a little.

When I began writing down my plans, working backward from my expected discharge date, I gradually reached a point where I could think:

"If I don't know what will happen, all I can do is accept this time as it comes and move forward."

Little by little, I began to regain something closer to my usual state of mind.

---

## The Anxiety of Having Nothing to Do

*Tests, Preparations, and Waiting*

After that, I was admitted to the hospital twice for examinations. During the second admission, preparations for surgery began.

At the time, however, only a limited number of hospitals had experience with laparoscopic surgery, so I had to wait two to three months before the operation.

That period of waiting, when there was nothing I could do, was the hardest part.

I spent my days worrying that the tumor might grow larger or begin affecting other parts of my body.

If I could have taken some kind of action, I might have been able to distract myself. But having no choice except to wait made the anxiety even harder to bear.

---

## The Day of Surgery: Time Passing Quietly

*The Rest Was in Their Hands*

The day of surgery marked my third hospital admission.

The anxiety I had felt while waiting had not disappeared. However, the preparations and activity leading up to the operation seemed to help take my mind off it.

During the final preparations before surgery, I remember taking a laxative and suddenly thinking, "This might be the last thing I ever remember."

At that moment, I decided to act as normally as possible, hoping to make things a little easier for myself emotionally.

One way I did this was by joking around as I was taken to the operating room and even in the moments just before the anesthesia took effect.

---

## After Surgery: Time in the ICU

*Bright Lights, Voices Calling My Name*

The next thing I remember was waking up beneath the bright lights of the ICU.

It felt as though only a moment had passed, but I later learned that the operation had actually taken many hours.

The surgery had been completed successfully.

I did not feel that I had accomplished anything in particular. All I remembered was the sensation of having been asleep.

I felt that I had made it through the operation thanks to the skill and dedication of the medical staff.

My honest reaction when I first woke up was, "I still want to sleep. Just leave me alone."

Looking back now, more than 20 years later, I realize that those bright lights and voices were the result of the medical team's work during nearly 10 hours of surgery.

My recovery began in the ICU.

I spent almost 24 hours a day in a brightly lit environment. Although I could feel that I was recovering, it was also a time when I found it difficult to relax.

The pain was not as severe as I had expected. What stayed with me more was a general sense of discomfort.

In particular, I noticed an unfamiliar sensation at the back of my head. At the time, I was told that it was not considered a major problem.

Several tubes were connected to my body, and I could not move freely. Even turning over in bed required care.

I remember looking at my family's expressions and realizing all over again just how serious my situation was.

---

## Recovery and Unexpected Changes

*Getting Better, but Trying to Get Back to Normal*

After a few days, my condition stabilized and I was transferred to a general hospital ward.

From that point on, the focus was on regaining my strength and undergoing rehabilitation in preparation for discharge.

Once I was allowed to walk, I began moving around within my limits. Little by little, I felt as though I was returning to everyday life.

However, after the anesthesia wore off, I sometimes experienced severe pain when getting up. It was not a recovery that could be described as entirely smooth.

I also began experiencing headaches that I had not had before surgery.

On one occasion, while I was walking, my vital signs suddenly became abnormal. My blood pressure, blood glucose, and oxygen levels dropped significantly.

I felt an unusual sensation in my chest and an intense sense of anxiety that I could not explain.

The situation improved after treatment. However, I came to understand that my body's balance would not immediately return to normal after surgery. I would need to wait for my condition to stabilize while it was being managed with medication.

As a result, my hospital stay was extended.

Eventually, I wasdischarged with a plan to continue medication and regular medical examinations.

Fortunately, no major problems, such as metastasis, were identified.

Feeling Different After Leaving the Hospital

Back Home, but Not Back to Normal

On the day I was discharged, I felt relieved that I could finally go outside again.

Since I was no longer under dietary restrictions, I decided to have a meal with my family on the way home.

However, while we were eating, I was suddenly overcome by severe fatigue, even though it was still early in the afternoon.

It was so difficult that I could barely remain seated without supporting my body.

That day, I assumed I was simply tired from being out after such a long time in the hospital, and I went home.

But afterward, I would repeatedly experience the same kind of overwhelming fatigue.

The Reality of Not Returning to My Old Normal

Beyond My Old Normal

At the time, I did not know what was causing these symptoms or how to deal with them.

I had no choice but to change the way I lived according to my physical condition.

Of course, not everyone experiences this kind of situation. The extent of recovery after surgery can vary greatly from person to person.

This brings my account of the journey from diagnosis to treatment to a close.

But my process of trial and error did not end here.

In many ways, this was only the beginning of a long journey of learning to live with changes in my quality of life.

Twenty Years Later, Still Doing Fine

Twenty Years Later, Still Doing Fine

As I write this, nearly 20 years have passed since those events.

In my case, I never fully returned to the physical condition I had before becoming ill.

Even so, I have been able to find ways to live with my condition by making small adjustments along the way.

At the time, I was trying to get back to my old normal. What I eventually came to understand was that the goal was not to return to the past, but to find a new normal.

I feel that being able to live my everyday life and enjoy it today is thanks to the medical professionals who cared for me and the understanding of the people around me.

Finding What Works for You

Finding What Works for You

As I grow older, I expect to face new challenges alongside the changes that come with age.

However, my experiences have helped me develop a feeling that, in most cases, things will somehow work out.

If you are going through a similar situation right now, it is okay if you cannot find the answers immediately.

By gradually discovering what works for you, I believe you can increase the amount of time you are able to spend feeling more comfortable than you do today.

I hope to continue sharing the small adjustments and insights I have gained through my experiences on this site.
