# Gradually Realizing Something Was Different

*Recognizing Changes in Memory and Concentration*

I noticed soon after being discharged from the hospital that my memory and concentration were no longer what they used to be.

Even when I tried to learn something new, the information often seemed difficult to absorb. Sometimes, I could not recall something I was sure I had learned just the day before. My condition also fluctuated considerably throughout the day. By the afternoon, my fatigue would become more pronounced, and I frequently found myself able to read the words on a page without actually taking in their meaning.

At the time, I could not tell whether these difficulties were caused by fatigue, drowsiness, or changes in my vital signs, such as blood pressure. As a result, I often found myself blaming myself and wondering, "Why can't I do this today?"

I began by using notes to help me manage my daily life, while continuing to observe what kinds of conditions might help restore my ability to concentrate.

Through repeated trial and error, I have now developed a set of methods that generally work for me when my concentration begins to decline in the afternoon.

## A Small Experiment That Made a Difference

One afternoon, at around 2:30 p.m., I was reading some documents when I found myself in my familiar state: I could follow the words on the page, but almost none of the information was registering. I was also beginning to feel as though my awareness might fade out.

I tried washing my face with cold water and continuing to read, but nothing improved. I could not tell whether I was lacking carbohydrates or simply feeling sleepy.

So I decided to try a short session of EEL, a relaxation method I use when I need to recover my concentration.

EEL involves lying down with earplugs and an eye mask, placing a cushion under my knees, and resting for about 15 minutes while consciously allowing my body to relax and my parasympathetic nervous system to become more active.

Since I thought I might fall asleep, I set an alarm for 20 minutes and lay down.

In fact, I seem to have fallen asleep briefly, and the alarm woke me up.

After getting up, I washed my face with cold water once again, started a stopwatch, and began reading the documents.

The difference was immediately apparent. This time, the information began to register naturally in my mind.

The sensation that my awareness might fade away was gone, and I was able to maintain my concentration for approximately 60 minutes.

I couldn't help raising my fist in a small gesture of triumph.

Rather than simply accepting that I could not concentrate because I was tired, I had observed my condition and conducted a series of small experiments. As a result, I had found one method that could help restore my concentration.

## Learning Through Small Experiments

I now feel that this method works reasonably well for me.

Going forward, I plan to investigate whether a second EEL session later in the day can restore my concentration in the same way, and to continue exploring how best to use it according to my own condition.

It does not work every time.

Even so, those moments when a small experiment succeeds and I find myself raising my fist in triumph encourage me to try the next hypothesis.

Perhaps, for me, improving my quality of life is not about experiencing some great miracle. It may simply be about accumulating these small moments of success, one after another.
