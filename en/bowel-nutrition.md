
# Learning How to Eat After Experiencing a Bowel Obstruction

I learned that some people experience a bowel obstruction after adrenal surgery.

In my case, about one to two years after my adrenalectomy, appendicitis progressed to peritonitis, and I developed a bowel obstruction. Fortunately, it resolved on its own. Even so, I sometimes still feel discomfort in my abdomen if I eat too much high-fiber food at one time.

When I was discharged from the hospital, I wasn’t given a list of foods I had to avoid. Later, when I sought medical attention because I was experiencing symptoms that felt like a possible bowel obstruction, my doctor explained that shungiku, or garland chrysanthemum, is a relatively fibrous vegetable and that eating a large amount at once can be hard on the intestines. The doctor also told me that no foods needed to be completely off-limits, as long as I adjusted the amount I ate.

So I began trying different foods in small amounts and gradually worked out what felt manageable for me.

At present, I have set approximate portion sizes for two foods in particular:

- **Corn:** about one-third of an ear
- **Shungiku (garland chrysanthemum):** about one-quarter of a bunch

With portions around this size, I can get the nutrients I need and feel comfortable eating them.

I’ve also tried various other high-fiber foods, and I haven’t had any particular problems when eating them in typical single-meal portions.

What I’ve come to realize is that **how I eat matters as much as what I eat.**

These days, I try to:

- Chew thoroughly, aiming for about 30 chews per bite
- Avoid eating a large amount at once
- Drink enough fluids
- Avoid eating a large amount of fiber on an empty stomach
- Take it easy when I’m not feeling well

Since I began paying attention to these habits, I feel that I’ve been putting much less strain on my intestines.

The most important thing for me wasn’t finding foods I must never eat. It was learning how much of each food I could eat without causing discomfort, and finding a portion size that works for me.

The right amount varies from person to person, depending on their body and medical history.

That’s why I believe observing the small signals from my body and learning what feels safe for me are important ways to continue enjoying food with greater peace of mind.

I used to think, “I’m healthy, so I can eat anything.” Looking back, I realize that I was living without really understanding my body.

Now, I pay attention to its small signals and choose portions and eating habits accordingly. This might look like having more restrictions, but I feel that I’ve actually gained more control over what I can do. That sense of reassurance is much greater than it used to be.
