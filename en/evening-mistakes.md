
# Estimating Daily Fatigue from Morning Changes in Blood Pressure and Pulse

After adrenalectomy, my morning blood pressure and pulse can vary considerably from day to day.

I now record my blood pressure and pulse each morning, from the time I wake up until about three hours later. I’m observing how much they change and whether those changes are related to how fatigued I feel in the afternoon.

This is not a medically established finding. It is simply a hypothesis based on my own observations. Still, I’ve noticed that **on days when my morning readings fluctuate more, a strong, “running out of battery” kind of fatigue tends to set in earlier in the afternoon.**

I don’t decide when to rest based on blood pressure and pulse alone. How I feel that day is also an important guide.

For example, if I notice that I have less emotional breathing room than usual, feel inexplicably rushed, or can’t settle my thoughts, I try to take a break before I become tired. When circumstances allow, I sometimes take a short break about once every two hours.

On days like these, taking even a brief break early can often help me maintain my ability to think and concentrate later on.

Using both my blood pressure and pulse records and the small changes I notice in my body and mind to adjust my rest schedule has become one of the ways I help maintain my quality of life.

I observe how I’m feeling not only through numbers, but also by describing my condition in my own words.

For example, when my mind feels hectic and I begin to lose my sense of emotional space, I describe it as “getting all wound up.” On days like these, taking frequent, short breaks before the fatigue becomes intense often helps me preserve my ability to think and concentrate.

On other days, my body and mind feel heavy, as though I’m being pulled toward a more withdrawn and passive state. I describe this feeling as “sinking.” When I feel this way, I’m more likely to turn inward, so I deliberately seek out some stimulation from the outside world. I might take a walk through a busy part of town or watch something bright and lively, such as an Indian film. For me, this is another way of keeping myself in a better mood.

For me, the numbers on my blood pressure monitor and pulse readings are not the only important clues. These signals from my body and mind also help me decide how to spend the day.

# When the Evening Battery Runs Low, Prepare in the Morning

*Let Your Morning Self Help Your Evening Self*

By evening, I can feel as though my battery has run out and I can no longer get things done.

In my case, I’ve rarely been able to overcome this daily fluctuation through willpower alone.

So I came up with a different approach: use the time when I feel well in the morning to make things easier for my evening self.

Before leaving home in the morning, I prepare as much as I can for the work I plan to do later. I lay out the necessary documents on my desk and open the relevant screens on my computer. Before heading out, I try to create a situation where I can start as soon as I get home.

I also read any documents that require careful attention at a coffee shop near the station.

At home, I often find it difficult to concentrate on dense material. But at the café, I naturally think, “I’ve paid for this coffee, so I might as well focus and make sure I understand this before I leave.” It feels as though I’m paying to borrow an environment that gives me a little extra incentive to stay focused.

Once I’ve absorbed the material, I walk home for about 20 minutes.

That walk is more than just a way to get home.

As I walk, I organize the material in my mind. My thoughts gradually come together, and the walk becomes a warm-up for the work ahead.

When I get home, I go straight to my desk without even changing my clothes first.

Rather than thinking, “I’m starting now,” I try to ease into the work by telling myself, “I actually started while I was walking home from the station.”

Since I began using this approach, I’ve had more days when I can make good use of the roughly two hours in the evening that I had previously given up on.

I can’t change the daily fluctuations in my condition through willpower alone.

That’s why, instead of trying to force my tired self to work harder, I’ve started looking for ways my more energetic self can help my tired self later in the day.

In my case, it takes this much planning of my surroundings and routine before I can manage these daily fluctuations.

Perhaps improving quality of life isn’t about finding ways to push ourselves harder. It may be about creating a flow that helps us get started naturally, without having to force ourselves.

# Later Reflection | I Meant to Do Just a Little in the Morning—and Finished Everything

*I was trying to help myself later in the day.*

There was an unexpected later development with this approach.

As preparing in the morning for my evening tasks became a habit, I found that I began to look forward to that time.

“Let me get things ready so that my self ten hours from now will have an easier time.”

I hadn’t expected that planning ahead with this thought in mind would feel so positive.

One morning, I had a thought:

“Wouldn’t it be even easier for my evening self to get started if I did just a little of the actual work now, instead of only preparing for it?”

So I began working on a task that I had originally planned to start in the evening.

I intended to make only a little progress. But the time limit before I had to leave, combined with my fresh morning mind, helped me focus more than I expected.

Before I knew it, I had finished the entire task that I had planned to do that evening.

That’s when I realized something.

I had assumed that I couldn’t get much done in the evening because I was tired.

Of course, that was part of the reason. But I also began to see that the moment I thought, “I’ll do it later,” I was losing some of my momentum.

That day, I changed my plan to work after returning home in the evening and went straight to a large public bath instead.

The reason was simple.

I hadn’t managed to set aside enough time before leaving in the morning to plan out the next task. (Laughs.)

Now, I’ve moved one step beyond the idea that “the morning is there to help my evening self.”

I’ve come to think that:

“Getting done in the morning whatever I can do in the morning makes the whole day more satisfying.”
